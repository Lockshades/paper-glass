# Business Model and Ecosystem

**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Working hypothesis  
**Last updated:** 2026-07-27

## Value creation

ExamPilot creates value by combining:

- realistic practice;
- reliable academic content;
- explanations that reduce confusion;
- analytics that turn scores into study actions;
- feedback that improves future content;
- a workflow that can eventually reward high-quality academic contributors.

## Actors

| Actor | Need | Value created | Potential value received |
|---|---|---|---|
| Student | Better preparation and confidence | Practice data, feedback, demand signal | Learning progress, explanations, readiness |
| Teacher or tutor | Better visibility into learner gaps | Context, review, teaching input | Student outcomes, tools, revenue opportunity |
| Academic reviewer | A structured way to validate content | Correctness and standards | Reputation, compensation, professional contribution |
| Content author | A way to create useful material | Questions, explanations, metadata | Attribution, compensation, reach |
| Tutorial centre | Scalable practice and cohort insight | Distribution and institutional demand | Tools, analytics, retention |
| University or partner | Better preparation outcomes | Credibility and distribution | Partnership value and insight |
| ExamPilot | Sustainable platform | Coordination, software, analytics | Revenue and defensible content/learning data |

## Business model hypotheses

The following options should be tested rather than assumed:

1. student subscription for premium practice and tutoring;
2. paid exam packs or one-time practice bundles;
3. tutorial-centre or school subscriptions;
4. institution-sponsored access;
5. carefully governed contributor marketplace fees or revenue sharing.

Advertising is not a first-choice foundation because it can conflict with focus, trust, and student privacy.

## Growth flywheel

```text
More students practice
  → richer performance evidence
  → better topic diagnostics
  → more useful revision
  → stronger outcomes and referrals
  → more students
```

The content flywheel is separate:

```text
AI-assisted drafts
  → academic review
  → approved question library
  → student usage and feedback
  → item quality evidence
  → better review and revision
```

## Economic guardrails

The initial business must measure:

- acquisition cost by channel;
- activation rate;
- practice-session completion;
- free-to-paid conversion, if applicable;
- retention by exam cohort;
- support cost;
- content creation and review cost per approved item;
- AI cost per active student;
- gross margin by product tier.

Do not present a unit-economic forecast as fact until it is based on observed usage, measured infrastructure costs, and a documented pricing test.

## Moats that may compound

- trusted exam-aligned content with provenance;
- student performance data connected to topic taxonomy;
- review and contributor reputation systems;
- institution workflows built around longitudinal learning evidence;
- accumulated evaluation data for AI explanations and recommendations.

The moat is not “we use AI.” AI access is broadly available. The defensible combination is trusted content, learning context, workflow, and evidence.

## Non-goals for the first commercial test

- building every examination body at once;
- launching a public marketplace before quality and payment controls exist;
- promising adaptive testing before enough validated item data exists;
- using projected millions of users to justify premature infrastructure complexity.

## Commercial questions requiring evidence

- Which student segment pays first?
- Does a realistic CBT experience or the AI tutor drive initial conversion?
- Which distribution channel has the lowest qualified acquisition cost?
- What minimum content breadth makes the product useful for one exam?
- Will tutorial centres pay for analytics before students pay directly?
